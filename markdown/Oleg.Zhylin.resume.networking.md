## My Story

I build the parts of a connected system that a backend engineer usually inherits. In **Python**, **Go**, **Java** or **.NET** the runtime already schedules the work and speaks the protocol. On an embedded device the engineer *is* that runtime, and for most of thirty years that has been my half of the problem.

I started as a **Data Security** and **Cryptography** engineer as an undergrad, working for mentors from my University department, on a **Client-Bank** system's cryptography. It left me a habit I have never lost: reading every system, and every protocol, from the standpoint of a **Security Professional**.

From **2000 to 2017** at [Salford Systems][salford] — later a [Minitab][minitab] company — I was an architect and technical lead in **Machine Learning/AI** long before the mainstream caught up, and the work I keep coming back to is the systems work. In **2004** I designed and single-handedly built a cross-platform **Client-Server** predictive-analytics system: a **TCP/IP daemon** with its own wire protocol, running concurrent analytics jobs on behalf of remote analysts, on **Windows**, **Linux** and **Solaris**. I learned it from [W. Richard Stevens' *UNIX Network Programming*][stevens_unp], which a colleague carried over to me in Ukraine and which I still consider the reference for writing network code. I managed teams up to **15 people** across the U.S., Ukraine, Poland and China.

Since **2018** at [GreatCall][greatcall] (presently [Best Buy Health][bbh]) I have built **Embedded** cellular devices for Seniors, at an **MVNO** that runs the whole pipeline from hardware manufacturing through to Care. On a device like this the network is never assumed healthy: the interesting engineering is what the product concludes and does when a broker is unreachable, a handshake fails, or the data path is simply gone. I built the fleet **Observability** practice on the other end of that link, found the individual devices quietly burning cellular data cost, and since **April 2026** I own **Architecture** decisions across both device lines while staying hands-on.

I have never specialized in networks, and I have come to think that is an advantage rather than a gap. My understanding of network *engineering* — as distinct from network *programming* — comes largely from **Russ White**, whose [Rule 11 Reader][rule11] is one of my beacons, and who taught me to read a domain as a short list of problems with a known solution space rather than as a stack of layers. I would like to take that into a networking specialization now, and I expect to arrive without the received practice that people who grew up inside it have to unlearn.

## Most prominent achievements

* ***Network programming from the wire up***. In **2004** I designed the entire system and implemented a fully cross-platform **TCP/IP daemon**: accept loop, hand-designed wire protocol, authentication, per-job threading, job isolation and lifetimes, progress reported back over the wire, and survival when a client disappeared mid-job. This was before `std::thread` and before reaching for **Boost.Asio** was the default, on three operating systems with three different socket and threading personalities. I organized it so that **one set of shared sources built both client and server**, which is the structural answer to the classic failure of a hand-rolled protocol: the two ends cannot drift if there is only one definition. It shipped on **Windows**, **Linux** and **Sun Solaris** with the native `.RPM`, `.DEB` and `.PKG` installers I authored. **[Boost.Asio][boost_asio]** is my day-to-day abstraction now, and I value it precisely because I built its hand-rolled equivalent first.
* ***Transport on a device that cannot assume the network is healthy***. On a cellular medical-alert device I work across the whole transport surface: asynchronous **HTTP** on **Boost.Beast** over a socket abstraction that serves plain **TCP** and **TLS** alike, with asynchronous **DNS** resolution, peer and hostname verification, **SNI**, and a timer-backed **TLS** shutdown. I corrected a **callback-ownership** defect in that shared transport, where an early local failure delivered its error through a moved-from callable instead of the owning object — the ownership model was right and the exceptional paths were using the wrong object. The part I care about more is the reasoning above the transport. The device's self-reported error path is a **fire-and-forget HTTPS POST**: it returns no delivery result, so a quiet monitoring dashboard is not evidence of a healthy device, and I distinguish an application's local failure, a failed attempt to report it, and the loss of a channel, because those three demand different operational conclusions.
* ***Traffic that has to pay for itself***. Every byte on this fleet is a direct operating cost, because the company is its own **MVNO**. I implemented and refined a reduced-activity **MQTT keep-alive** mode, and the design principle that made it work was separating the **schedule** from the **work scheduled** — stopping the timer indiscriminately would also remove the chances to do required maintenance and advance the communication cycle, so the scheduler keeps running while optional dispatch is gated at the component boundaries. I kept the **file-descriptor** health check ahead of that suppression, because a reduced-activity mode is not permission to disable the checks that keep a process able to open a socket at all. Diagnostic traffic is bounded the same way: the first occurrence of an exact error is sent and repeats are counted for a later summary.
* ***Degrading instead of disappearing***. The device keeps working when its **TCP/IP** data path does not. An unavailable broker session is not an unreachable device: there is a separate, narrower **SMS** command path, so the product degrades to a reduced set of communication capabilities rather than going dark. Designing for that means being precise about which observation proves what — voice-service registration, cellular packet-data availability, **TCP** connectivity, **TLS** authentication and broker session health are five different facts, and I built the device's low-signal indication from **RSRP**, **RSSI**, **RSRQ** and **SNR** deliberately *not* as an end-to-end reachability test.
* ***Publish/subscribe between processes, over UDP***. Most of my career is **TCP/IP**, as most of the internet is. The recent counterweight is [**LCM** — Lightweight Communications and Marshalling][lcm_proj], the open-source messaging library from the robotics world, carried over **UDP multicast**. I took a capability **SDK** onto production-class hardware as two processes talking over three named channels — commands out, events back, and a third carrying log records across the process boundary so device logging stays unified across an organizational boundary — with **JSON** payloads and a test node that subscribes to the live channels and pumps messages under bounded timeouts, so the multi-process conversation is covered by automated tests rather than by manual bring-up. Choosing a proven library with a multicast transport over a bespoke socket protocol keeps the boundary **debuggable**: any process on the device can subscribe and watch the conversation, which is exactly what the tests do.
* ***Fleet telemetry, and finding the devices that misbehave***. I brought together data that had never been in the same place — **cellular-operations data** from the network operator, **device telemetry** from the fleet, and the warehouse's own event tables, joined on device identity (**IMEI**) — to find individual units consuming cellular data far outside any plausible pattern. Rather than argue about a threshold, I wrote a **rogue-device emulation** script that drove deliberate excessive consumption, so detection could be validated against a device known to be misbehaving, and proposed the threshold *before* an incident rather than during one. I published the **runbook** so the response did not depend on me, fed the scenario into the launch **tabletop exercise**, and the work found a real unit shortly after it was built.
<!-- include: _parts/bullet-operational-excellence.md -->
* ***Location as the thing the transport serves***. Accurate **Location** is not a feature on an emergency-response device; it is the product. I became the company's **Subject Matter Expert on Positioning** (**GNSS** — GPS, GLONASS, Galileo — ECID, Wi-Fi, **BLE** beacons), and architected the **Location Engine** that unifies beacon, GPS and Wi-Fi behind per-provider interfaces, with a state-management layer that arbitrates between sources and falls back when one fails. The networking decision inside it is scheduling: location reports ride **MQTT** keep-alive wake-ups the device was making anyway, so position costs the battery and the data plan as little as possible.
* ***How I read a network***. My network *programming* comes from [Stevens][stevens_unp] and from having built a server from scratch. My network *engineering* understanding comes largely from **Russ White** — his [Rule 11 Reader][rule11] and his video courses — and specifically from his habit of describing a domain as a small set of problems, each with a known solution space. In his **"Four Things"** model those are **marshaling**, **multiplexing**, **flow control** and **error control**, offered deliberately as an alternative to memorizing a layer diagram. I use it as a working checklist, and I find it holds well outside networking too. I state the limit of this plainly: I have no **routing-protocol** or **switch-configuration** practice, and nothing in my record should be read as claiming any.
* ***Concurrency, which is the same skill***. A server is a concurrency problem wearing a socket. I diagnosed an intermittent race condition in a device's audio service **without ever reproducing it**, working from correlated logs that showed the same thread created twice and the same wakelock taken twice in one second, then designed the fault injection and instrumentation to pin it. I later drove a contract manufacturer's concurrency corrections round by round — a queue predicate checked outside its mutex, a peeked pointer used after unlock, shared stop state under inconsistent locks — requiring a documented lock order and structural soundness before stress testing counted as evidence.
* ***Architecture ownership**, and the seam between hardware and software*. Since **April 2026** I own **Architecture** decisions across both the **Wearables** ([Lively Mobile+][r4], [Lively Mobile 2][r5]) and **Handsets** lines, and I stay hands-on across all of them. A device with new hardware runs on a rigid cycle of six months or more while the software iterates inside it; my best results have come from **engineer-to-engineer** relationships with manufacturer, silicon and firmware partners across that seam.
* ***Management** and **Technical Leadership** of **Distributed** teams*. Coordinating **U.S.** developers with contractors in **Ukraine**, **Poland** and **China** is far from trivial; I managed teams up to **15 people**, and got my best results by **empowering others** and **leading from behind**.

### 2023-2026. Device transport: HTTP, MQTT, and what happens when the network is gone.

I corrected callback ownership on the early failure paths of the shared asynchronous **HTTP** transport, where an error branch invoked a moved-from callable rather than the owning object, for both file-backed responses and file-backed requests. Around it I work with the properties that make this a real transport rather than a wrapper: a socket abstraction serving plain **TCP** and **TLS**, asynchronous resolution, connection and handshake, peer and hostname verification with **SNI**, timer-backed shutdown, and an operation policy above the transport that separates a successful response, a retryable one, a terminal one, a transport failure and an elapsed deadline, with a per-attempt observer arbitrating so only one path consumes an attempt's completion.

On the **MQTT** side I implemented a reduced-activity keep-alive mode, separating the periodic schedule from the optional work it dispatches, consolidating the configuration across the components that consume it, and adding the tests that assert a suppressed request is genuinely not sent. I kept the **file-descriptor** check ahead of the suppression, because losing the ability to open a socket is not a condition a low-activity mode should be allowed to hide.

I treat the failure surface explicitly: broker unavailable while IP still works, **DNS**/**TCP**/**TLS** failure, a stalled operation whose timeout depends on a working executor rather than on a hardware watchdog, and the case where the data path is gone entirely and the device continues on its **SMS** command path.

### 2023-2026. Concurrency on the device, and across a manufacturer boundary.

An emergency-response device occasionally played a flat beep instead of its voice prompt, sometimes followed by a reboot. I never reproduced it, so I went after the evidence instead: correlated logs showed the audio request handler creating its playback thread twice and taking its **wakelock** twice in the same second, immediately before the gap that marks a forced reboot, and an independent subsystem's reboot counter told the same story. I instrumented the device, designed **fault injection** to force the window open, and ran a shutdown-during-playback experiment whose *negative* result separated two failure modes that had looked identical.

In 2026 I carried that across the manufacturer boundary, into their **C**, **POSIX threads**, **GLib** and **ALSA** code, where the value lay entirely in reading it. I named each race precisely enough to be implemented, reviewed every returned submission, and required a documented lock order and structural soundness before stress testing counted as evidence. After the fixes, QA reported the service running smoothly.
