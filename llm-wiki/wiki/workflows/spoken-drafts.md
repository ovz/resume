# Spoken drafts — writing a story the owner can rehearse

> **Doc type:** how-to
>
> How to turn a story's structure into narrative lines that the owner can say aloud, rehearse, and recover from mid-sentence. Audience: any agent writing or revising the *Narrative* section of a story; the owner deciding why a draft does or does not sound like him.
>
> **This page sits under [voice and prominence](voice-and-prominence.md), never beside it.** That page governs register, sentence shape and prominence, and wins wherever the two disagree. This page adds the rehearsal craft that its rules imply but do not spell out. Structure and notation stay in [brag stories](brag-stories.md).

## The rules

1. **Lift, don't compose.** Where the owner has dictated the episode, the spoken lines are built from his sentences — reordered, trimmed, grammar fixed — rather than paraphrased into better prose. His dictation is the closest thing on record to how he talks when a memory is live; an agent's paraphrase is the fastest route to the corporate register the voice page bans. Mark a draft that could not do this ("no dictation on record; agent-drafted") so the owner knows which lines to replace with his own.
2. **Allow his sentence shape, not only short declaratives.** He narrates in cumulative sentences that carry a reason: a claim, *because*, a consequence, then an aside. Two clauses joined by *and*, *but*, *so* or *because* are his; a string of four-word sentences is not. The one-breath test from brag stories still applies.
3. **One of his own rules per story, owned as his.** He states principles as rules — *no product means no churn*; *if there is no book with the right answer, the right answer doesn't exist*; *pulling your own weight is a form of rent seeking*. These look like the epigrams the voice page bans, and the difference is provenance: the ban is on lines composed to be quoted, and his sayings are recorded in his own words. Use at most one, introduced as his ("The way I put it to myself is…"), with the reasoning around it, and **never as the closing line**. An agent never coins one on his behalf.
4. **One metaphor per story, explained in the same breath.** A physical, economic or mechanical comparison is his signature — the broken alternator for a descriptor leak, the perpetual-motion machine, Jevons's paradox. Say what it means in the same sentence, so a listener who does not know the reference still follows. Take it from his repertoire in the record; do not invent one.
5. **One humour beat, self-directed, in a low-stakes moment.** His warmest material is about himself — a word he misunderstood in his second language, a limit he admits with a smile. Never at a colleague's expense.
6. **A cue: the one concrete image from the record.** Each story names, under *Why I still care*, the thing he actually saw — a log line, a card on the board, a sentence in an email, a meeting. If the rehearsed text slips, the image puts him back in the room, and he can tell it from there. It is the practical form of the voice page's role-play rule.
7. **Mark memory once, and only where the record is uncertain.** "If I remember right" is honest and conversational; three of them sound unsure. Where the record has the date, say the date.
8. **End on what he delivered or what survived.** A punchline that is somebody else's decision — a freeze, a cancellation, an idea that "fell on deaf ears" — hands the listener the wrong ending. Say what he did, what he learned, or what outlasted him; put the decision in *If they follow up*.
9. **People get their generous line first.** Credit a colleague by role with one concrete trait before the point; the full account, names and friction included, lives in *Know it*.
10. **Fix the grammar he will say.** Articles, prepositions and capitalised common nouns are corrected in the draft, because rehearsing a slip ingrains it. His own quoted words elsewhere in the record stay verbatim.

## A rehearsal pass

Before marking a story `status: rehearsed`, the owner (or an agent reading it back to him) checks:

- Read *Why I still care* and the cue, then say the story once **without looking**. Where it drifts, the draft is either too written or missing a step.
- Say it once at full length and once at forty seconds, dropping every `*(optional)*` line. Both must land every beat.
- Mark any line he stumbled on twice. It is rewritten, not re-rehearsed.

## What not to copy from the dictation

His dictated notes are raw record, and several things in them never reach a spoken line: verdicts on colleagues; criticism of an employer stated as fact; several stories stacked in one paragraph (split them); and the ending that hands the outcome to someone else. The private account keeps all of it, in full — see [sensitivity tiers](sensitivity-tiers.md) rule 8.
