# Built a standing practice of giving structured, specific feedback upward to my manager

## What I did

I kept two running lists for my manager — positive feedback and critical feedback — and brought items from them into one-on-ones deliberately, rather than only answering the questions I was asked. Most engineers receive feedback; far fewer prepare and deliver it upward on a cadence.

**The critical feedback was specific, actionable, and paired with a proposal:**

- **Information vacuum.** I said I felt disconnected from what was happening with his peers and across the organization, that I doubted he could over-supply me with that information, and that I liked a lot of detail in my big picture. The proposal was concrete: keep us informed, because it makes the team feel connected — and I tied it to a goal of my own, wanting to be better connected across the company and to make better progress at building relationships with individuals and teams. Pointed at recent positive cross-team interaction from locations research and a happy hour with the data-science team as proof the direction worked, and suggested joint learning sessions as the next step.
- **Process honesty — Kanban versus Scrum.** I agreed with his own view that our work was really Kanban rather than Sprint, and pushed that agreeing was not enough: it would not change until we actively tailored the process to the work we did, and scrum masters were happy to follow the letter of the process. Proposed *kanbanizing* development and forming a sprint only when there was a backlog worth going underground for two weeks to execute. Also told him directly that he was giving a colleague too much slack in clinging to sprint rituals for their own sake.
- **Missing a BHAG.** I named a substantial lack of a Big Hairy Audacious Goal, said he could be more aggressive about getting one for the team, and made the argument: to win big we need to play big, and at any stage of the project we can work out what we can excel at.
- **Decision-making structure.** I observed that decisions often seemed ready to be made while everyone felt awkward taking the decisive step, and asked for better structure: when brainstorming, identify each suggested solution; at the end, one person makes the decision. He might need to be that person, or explicitly designate a judgement-caller.
- **Self-deprecation is counter-productive.** I told him that phrases like "push back if you disagree" and "I'm rambling" had the opposite effect to the one intended. My preference: start from the assumption that everyone *is* free to speak up, and instead keep a vigilant eye that everyone who could contribute actually has.
- Also raised the unglamorous but real one — a learning-platform subscription I had asked for and not received.

**The positive feedback was equally specific**, and not merely polite:

- Thanked him for being diligent as the face of the company, naming why it was hard: he was the face regardless of what he did, and his behaviour only determined *what kind* of face it was.
- Told him I valued his insights, and that I was glad when my own developing intuition matched his — which locates the value precisely rather than flattering.
- Reinforced a shared goal: that I wanted to get excellent at hiring, and asked him to keep setting the bar high.
- Named a specific social occasion that had worked — shared experiences and recollections at a happy hour — and asked for more of it.

## Why it matters

- **Managing upward is a staff-level behaviour, and this is the evidence of it** done as a sustained practice rather than a one-off escalation.
- **Every criticism carried a proposal.** None of these is a complaint; each identifies a mechanism and suggests a replacement, which is what makes upward feedback usable rather than merely honest.
- **The Kanban and decision-making items are process design**, argued from how the work actually behaved rather than from process orthodoxy.
- **The positive feedback is calibrated**, naming what was hard about the thing being praised, which makes it credible and makes the critical feedback land better.
- It demonstrates a working relationship with enough trust in it to say difficult things in both directions — and this manager remains a listed professional reference.

## Skills demonstrated

Managing upward; giving specific and actionable feedback; process design and agile judgement; organizational communication; building trust through candour; hiring interest and bar-setting.
