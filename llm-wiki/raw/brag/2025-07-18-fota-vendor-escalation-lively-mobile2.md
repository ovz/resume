# Became the FOTA subject-matter expert in record time during a device-inventory crisis, and led the vendor escalation

## What I did

The device programme went red. Inventory was projected to run out in December, factory firmware had to be delayed, and the cause was firmware-over-the-air update failure — the responsibility of the ODM who manufactured the device and owned its firmware, working with a specialist FOTA vendor whose technology updates a very large share of devices worldwide. Between them the update process for the device was broken.

**Became the subject-matter expert on FOTA in record time.** I went well beyond what my role required to build genuine depth in firmware-over-the-air update mechanics, because the alternative was negotiating with two vendors from a position of ignorance — both of whom were nominally the experts.

**Led the technical side of the vendor negotiation.** That expertise is what made the escalation work: being able to argue about implementation details with an ODM and a specialist FOTA vendor on their own ground, rather than accepting assurances.

**Drove it to a resolution I could defend to the business while flagging its engineering cost honestly.** The outcome was adequate for the business goal and not to my engineering liking, and I recorded both halves. Specifically: the team ended up with more than fifty FOTA test protocols, which are brittle because we were effectively performing the testing the ODM should have done and inspecting their implementation details to do it. I named that as technical debt at the time it was incurred rather than discovering it later.

**Held the team steady through an all-hands-on-deck period.** Day to day I kept the team grounded, helped QA engineers feel appreciated and productive, and set well-grounded expectations with leaders at several levels — deliberately aiming for less chaos and more visible steps toward resolution. I noted the specific failure mode I was guarding against: under pressure, people tend not to be careful enough about maintaining the confidence stakeholders already have, while chasing the fix.

**Drew the organizational lesson without deflecting.** My honest assessment was that much of this might have been avoidable had I been properly involved in the original decision-making — and rather than treat that as a grievance, I used it as an argument for being in those rooms. I worked the situation through a deliberately stoic frame: separate what depends on me from what does not, and get inside the OODA loop.

**Used the crisis as growth material.** I took it into my mentorship sessions explicitly as an opportunity for principal-engineer behaviours, and reported the quarter's accomplishments against it rather than around it.

**Noted the cross-pollination.** The parallel data-catalog and data-governance work fed into the FOTA effort and kept me visible across teams and departments — an unplanned benefit of holding a stretch assignment outside my core area.

## Why it matters

- **Rapid, deliberate acquisition of deep domain expertise under time pressure**, undertaken specifically to change the balance of a vendor negotiation, is a principal-level move rather than an engineering one.
- **It was a genuine business-critical event** — inventory exhaustion in a fixed number of months on a product for vulnerable users, with a hard, unmovable need for the device.
- **The technical-debt honesty is the valuable part.** Recording that fifty-plus brittle test protocols are debt, and *why* (we are doing the ODM's job and coupling to their implementation), at the moment of incurring it, is what separates a fix from a liability that surfaces later.
- **Leadership without authority over the vendors**, and steadying a team while managing expectations upward, is the behaviour the career goal at the time was defined by.
