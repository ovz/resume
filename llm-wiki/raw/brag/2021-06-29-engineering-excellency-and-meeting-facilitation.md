# Made Engineering Excellency an explicit team goal, and defended meeting facilitation as a discipline

## What I did

I carried Engineering Excellency as a named professional principle and an explicit OKR goal — improving our engineering as a team — and pursued it through the unglamorous mechanisms where it actually lives: meeting discipline, triage, and how optional quality work gets scheduled.

**Started the triage meeting.** Organizing it was my own initiative, and I used the triage queue as the instrument for deciding whether optional engineering-quality items made it into the release.

**Made the scheduling argument that optional work decays.** My position, confirmed by our own experience: defer the decision on optional items and within a month or so *all* of them drift into the "too risky" category and are never done. So the decision should be made while there is full information — decide whether we want any optional items, then find a way to fit them into the schedule. I asked my manager directly for support in bringing in what was useful, rather than letting the default of deferral quietly decide.

**Raised a facilitation problem directly with my manager, and did it well.** He had overruled the agenda of a triage meeting I was facilitating. I deliberately waited until he was back from medical leave rather than raising it in the moment, then made a careful, specific case:

- Established the shared facts first — that I was the facilitator — before asking for his reasoning.
- Offered to examine my own meeting invitation together to check whether the agenda had been unclear, having directed people to the board and actively invited collaboration beforehand.
- Made the general argument: an agenda is something known *before* a meeting; overruling it on the spot should be exceptional; and in most cases even a bad agenda beats staying in the meeting and playing it by ear. Better to stick to the agreed agenda and dismiss the meeting early to regroup than to improvise.
- Named the second-order damage, which was the real point: when the facilitator is overruled, other team members conclude they can override the agenda any time and for any reason — which is corrosive to every future meeting.
- Made a personal ask with a concrete alternative: give me that feedback *after* the meeting, because I do not feel comfortable expressing disagreement while facilitating.
- Stated the ideal outcome plainly — sync before the meeting and cancel it if needed.

**Extended the same standard to meeting invitations generally:** I would far rather someone decline a meeting I called with constructive objections than have everyone assemble and then work out why we were there.

**Held myself to the same principles and asked to be checked against them.** I named Humility, Respect and Trust as the foundation I worked from, and keeping ego separate from work matters as a professional behaviour — then asked my manager directly for any example where I had deviated from either. Asking to be shown your own violations is a different act from asserting the principle.

**Learned from a friction episode and said what changed.** After a design and triage disagreement, my stated takeaway was how important it is to be strictly professional about work matters: everyone's personality is unquestionably entitled to respect, and on that basis we drive toward the best outcomes. I recorded that disagreeing and committing is not a challenge for me, and used the episode as a communication opportunity — talking one-on-one with team members afterwards to bring everyone along gently rather than pressing the point in a group.

**Read the room on timing.** I deliberately did not push the Engineering Excellence agenda as hard as I wanted while my manager was dealing with ongoing insurance issues — the judgement that a good agenda pushed at the wrong moment is a worse agenda.

**Took ownership of task tracking.** Observing that a colleague carrying the architecture vision was overworked, I proposed taking ownership of proper task tracking in Jira and staying abreast of his architecture-board work — reasoning that tracking mattered more to the manager, other stakeholders and me than to him, and freeing him to spread the vision to the broader team.

**Gave the team-meeting feedback that asks for more, not less.** After one of the best team meetings we had had, I said so, then asked specifically for more *actionable questions* for the team: churn numbers were being presented without action items, and an engineering team wants to find good questions and has a compulsive predisposition to look for answers. Either tell us what question a stakeholder thinks is the important one, or ask us explicitly to discover it.

## Why it matters

- **Engineering quality is won or lost in scheduling and meeting mechanics**, not in advocacy. Naming that optional work decays into "too risky" within a month is a precise, testable claim about how quality work actually dies.
- **The facilitation conversation is a model of raising a grievance well** — timed considerately, framed around shared facts, offering to examine one's own contribution first, arguing the systemic consequence rather than the personal slight, and ending with a concrete alternative.
- **Asking to be shown my own deviations** from principles I had just stated is a genuinely uncommon move, and it is what makes stating them credible.
- **It is influence without authority throughout** — initiating triage, taking on tracking to unblock someone else, and requesting a change in a manager's behaviour.

## Skills demonstrated

Meeting facilitation and agenda discipline; triage process design; scheduling advocacy for engineering-quality work; managing upward; giving and requesting feedback; conflict de-escalation and one-on-one alignment; principled professional conduct; workload awareness across a team.
