# Entered a formal mentorship to reach Principal Engineer, and ran it as a structured working relationship

## What I did

I joined the enterprise mentorship programme with a specific, stated objective — become a Principal Engineer before fifty, with CTO-level responsibility as a declared stretch goal — and ran the relationship deliberately rather than passively receiving advice. This was the longest-running mentoring relationship in my records, and I maintained a standing list of topics to revisit across sessions so the conversation compounded instead of resetting.

**Set the terms explicitly at the start.** Stated my goal, and stated the reasoning behind the stretch ambition honestly: I first needed confirmation that others believed in me, and only then could polish CTO-relevant talents into something presentable. I also turned the question around and asked my mentor what *his* goals were for the collaboration, and what he had wanted when he joined the programme — treating it as a two-way working relationship rather than a service.

**Brought real, current problems** rather than abstract career questions — a firmware-over-the-air crisis, an incident bridge and who had not collaborated willingly enough, a data-governance stretch assignment, a reorganization with layoffs, and presentations that had gone imperfectly.

**Worked on specific, named weaknesses**, which is the part most career conversations avoid:

- **Projecting confidence.** Diagnosed the split precisely: rehearsal and delivery technique were improving through structured courses, but confidence *on the opinion side* was still lacking. The fix I identified was preparation, not personality — decide in advance what I agree and disagree with, front-load the engineering thinking, foresee alternatives, and be ready to defend a position. Worked from a live example where a product manager asserted that a data catalog could cover all business-intelligence needs; my conclusion was that I needed to be able to indicate, in the moment, that an alternative had not received proper engineering support.
- **Appearing sharp-elbowed.** Named this as my greatest challenge, acknowledged that my ways of overcompensating were not the worst but did not build trust, and identified the target directly: project humility, respect and trust — hardest and most necessary exactly when one of those feels threatened by someone else.
- **Pausing under pressure.** Built a concrete practice for the moment a conversation stops going as expected: pause, up to three deliberate breaths, orient on verbal and non-verbal cues, run an impromptu risk analysis, and be willing to say I will come back to someone — without appearing evasive.

**Tracked organizational reality alongside the personal goal.** Recorded that a reorganization with layoffs had happened and that being retained was information, not comfort; that the promotion path had shifted from "leave the organization to get promoted" to three internal principal promotions actually happening, which changed the strategy to pushing for the grade jump directly; and that a mentor-side peer was similarly overdue, doing comparable work, with the blocker being that decisions simply were not made.

**Named the strategy for driving work without authority:** gently signalling that I could do the work myself, so that people feel empowered either to ask for help or to move faster — while deliberately not confirming that something is valuable in a way that lets another team keep it as a second priority.

**Chose the direction deliberately.** Reaffirmed the individual-contributor track over management, and identified with the *expert generalist* framing — T-shaped expertise, argued to compound fastest as LLMs mature — concluding that expert generalists are well positioned to be the principal engineers of the future.

**Reported the results back.** The mentorship contributed directly to the best quarterly conversation I had had: two leaders instead of one, substantial encouragement, and goals made concrete and specifically useful for the upcoming promotion — and I told my mentor plainly that he had made the key difference.

## Why it matters

- **It is career development run as an engineering problem** — explicit goal, named weaknesses, concrete practices, tracked outcomes.
- **The weaknesses are specific and unflattering**, which is what makes the work on them real. "Project confidence" is generic; "my confidence gap is on the opinion side, and the fix is preparing my disagreements in advance" is actionable.
- **It shows organizational strategy, not just self-improvement** — reading how promotions actually happen in this organization and adjusting the approach accordingly.
- **The mentoring relationship itself is evidence of the level being targeted**: bringing live crises to a mentor and converting them into practice is what the transition from senior to principal actually consists of.
