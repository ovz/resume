# Navigated a performance challenge by reframing it as an organizational problem, and argued the promotion question honestly

## What I did

Under schedule pressure on the R5 MVP, my manager opened a conversation stating that my performance was off track. I prepared for it deliberately rather than reacting, and the preparation is the accomplishment.

**Refused the premise while accepting the standard.** My position was that off-track is unacceptable *to me as a professional* — so the disagreement was never about whether the bar mattered, only about whether it had been missed. I acknowledged the value of having my feet held to the fire while disagreeing that it was necessary, and conceded the one point that actually held: what matters is the productivity of the organization as a whole, not any individual's output.

**Reframed the conversation as a shared problem.** The line I prepared: that we started the performance conversation from the wrong foot, that we are on the same team with the same work ethic, that over the preceding two years our understanding had been right and our execution sound given the circumstances — and that the parts of the organization which had mismanaged the business into unprofitability were now putting *our* feet to the fire. The proposal that followed was collaboration on two horizons: tactically, get the MVP out of the door; strategically, establish a strong engineering culture and help business partners improve the things they could actually control.

**Made the accountability argument precisely.** Rather than deflecting, I named where the cost had actually been incurred — budget spent on product lines that lost money — against teams that were demonstrably reducing call-centre operating cost. The ask was not to escape scrutiny but to have good work acknowledged and be extended enough trust to find a way through the next quarter.

**Named the schedule's artificial constraints.** I pushed back that "fix it all" is an unreasonable expectation when engineers receive only trickles of updated information from planning that runs for weeks and months, and argued that with a senior engineering mind focused on getting the device done rather than serving other branches of the organization, a far more aggressive plan would have been possible. Noted concretely that I could have written considerably more beacon-tracking code against a mature, documented, peer-reviewed design.

**Addressed the promotion competition directly instead of letting it hang.** Two of us were plainly in contention for one Staff Engineer promotion, and I said so out loud. My argument: **nobody deserves a promotion.** An organization *earns* the opportunity to promote people by getting stronger. So the honest question was whether the business could support promoting both of us, or whether it should simply acknowledge that it was only strong enough for so many Staff Engineer roles — and my recommendation was to clarify which, rather than leave it ambiguous. I asked my manager plainly whether both of us being promoted was a realistic possibility.

**Prepared with tactical empathy.** I worked the conversation from the other side first — what does my manager actually think, and what would make them say *that's right* — rather than rehearsing my own case. That preparation also surfaced the uncomfortable readings I needed to hold as live possibilities: that a decision had already been made and a rationale was being constructed around it, and that pressure was a way of extracting more output. Holding those as hypotheses to test rather than conclusions to act on is what kept the conversation productive.

**Turned it into a communication question.** The most useful thing I took into the room was a genuine question rather than a defence: how can I communicate better that I am applying top engineering expertise to this? I committed to keep surfacing the non-trivial challenges I was discovering and how I was overcoming them — making the invisible work visible, which was the actual gap.

**Read the disagreement about process honestly.** Part of the friction traced to a disagreement I recorded separately: leadership promoting processes and tools as an agile first principle, when individuals and interactions come first and processes and tools explicitly second.

## Why it matters

- **This is the hardest kind of professional conversation, prepared for as an engineering problem** — hypotheses, evidence, the other party's perspective modelled first, and a proposal at the end.
- **"Promotions are decided, not deserved"** reframes a zero-sum competition into a question about organizational capacity, which is both more accurate and more actionable than an argument about individual merit.
- **It shows disagreement handled without either capitulating or becoming defensive**, and with the one valid point conceded explicitly.
- **The self-critical finding is the valuable one:** the real gap was that hard work on non-obvious problems was not visible to the person evaluating it, and the fix was communication, not effort.
- Recorded because it is part of the professional record. Career narratives that contain only wins are not credible, and how someone handles being told they are underperforming says more than another shipped feature.

## Skills demonstrated

Difficult conversations; tactical empathy and negotiation preparation; managing upward; organizational and political awareness; separating the valid part of criticism from the invalid; making invisible work visible; principled reasoning about promotion and reward systems; emotional regulation under pressure.
