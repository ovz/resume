# Ran a sustained self-assessment against the Staff Engineer job family, and used it to steer toward Principal

## What I did

I maintained a running, evidence-backed audit of my own work against the enterprise Engineering Individual Contributor job family — not as an annual review exercise, but as a standing list I revisited and added to over years, split honestly into what I had demonstrated and what I had not.

**On the demonstrated side**, mapped concrete evidence to each expectation:

- **Production support and operational excellence** — designed, developed and maintained it as an ongoing responsibility rather than a project.
- **Industry-trend engagement** — C++, AI, Rust, embedded development, blockchain, extreme programming and agile; sustained through an AI study group and a book club rather than passive reading.
- **Ensuring quality continuously** — framed as the first principle of my professional ethics, not a process step: pull-request review, promoting TDD and mentoring others in it, and repurposing tooling such as code coverage into metrics that actually drive behaviour. Separately recorded that code-coverage accounting as practised was flawed, which is the kind of thing that only surfaces when you use a metric seriously.
- **Working independently under incomplete information** — producing deliverables on time, bringing clarity, and moving the organization closer to its goals without waiting to be unblocked.
- **Cross-team relationships** — a track record of them, built deliberately.
- **Risk management** — a professional-development goal that kept paying off, reinforced by a data-security background.
- **Depth across multiple domains** — C, C++, Python, Rust, SQL; data engineering on Snowflake; operational excellence; cybersecurity.
- **Technical debt** — proactively spotting it, naming the trade-offs, and making sure everyone was aware of them; specifically treating long-lived feature branches as debt and teaching that.
- **Feedback beyond my own area** — built through a design-review process, noting it gave more visibility than the equivalent process elsewhere in the organization.
- **Deep architectural understanding of a major part of the business** — pursued deliberately as a self-set stretch goal and then exploited fully.

**On the gap side**, wrote the honest version rather than the flattering one, and distinguished gaps that were mine from gaps that were the organization's:

- Driving a large work stream in the engineering community of practice — attempted a presentation at the enterprise level and was politely declined; opportunities within the sub-organization were limited.
- Mentoring — constrained by there not being enough people to mentor, and by a team that lacked motivation to raise the bar.
- Owning an area of the codebase — pushed back on the framing itself, on the grounds that ownership phrased that way becomes a silo; deliberately contributed across multiple codebases instead and encouraged others to.
- Internal libraries and tools — noted these easily backfire as coupling, while acknowledging capability-SDK work did satisfy the expectation.
- Technology strategy — observed this sat with architects, that strategy appeared to flow top-down rather than being shared, and stated plainly that I wanted a seat at that table.
- Teaching — identified Rust for embedded as the topic I would lead with once priorities allowed.

**Turned the audit into positioning.** Recognized the *expert generalist* framing when it surfaced in industry commentary — T-shaped expertise, argued to be best positioned for value growth as LLMs mature — identified myself as a long-standing aspiring expert generalist, and concluded that expert generalists are well positioned to be the principal engineers of the future. Added it to how I present myself.

**Named the career goal and the obstacle explicitly:** to become a Principal Engineer before fifty, with a stretch ambition toward CTO-level responsibility; and the pay-grade arithmetic that goal implied. Recorded the frustration honestly — that results were consistently complimented, operational excellence in particular, without matching enthusiasm to build on the momentum; and that in years of assignments I had never once declined work or asked for something easier.

**Made the case in terms of what the organization controls:** if delivery was judged insufficient, then the organization should either set explicit goals such as an MVP schedule and reward achievement against them, or extend the latitude and forgiveness that lets an engineer deliver on their own initiative — pointing at beacon tracking, risk management, secure boot and patch management as things delivered exactly that way.

**Was clear about which track I wanted and why.** Rejected the middle-management path on principle rather than convenience: a people manager is the organization's avatar to their reports and professionally commits to carrying the organization's values, whichever they are, while the individual-contributor track leaves room to practise one's own. Framed against growing up in the Soviet Union, where individual values were not available, and delegating collective values to people better qualified to hold them.

**Sought coaching deliberately** — on the reasoning that every top performer has at least one coach, and taking the word in its original sense of a means of getting somewhere more easily.

## Why it matters

- **It is the paper trail behind a promotion case**, built continuously with evidence attached, rather than assembled retroactively when a review demanded it.
- **The gap analysis is unusually honest**, including gaps that reflect badly on the environment and gaps that reflect on me, which is what makes the demonstrated side credible.
- **It shows career direction being reasoned about, not drifted into** — an explicit target, an explicit rejection of the management track with an argued reason, and an explicit theory of what the next decade rewards.
- **It converts a grievance into a proposal.** The promotion frustration is real and recorded, but the framing it resolves to is about what the organization could do differently, which is the more useful artifact.

## Skills demonstrated

Self-assessment against a competency framework; career strategy; evidence-based advocacy; honest gap analysis; organizational awareness; principled career-track reasoning; sustained multi-year record-keeping.
